Project 01 / Thermal Systems / Motorsport

Keeping the driver
cool enough to think.

A lightweight thermal-management concept for motorsport, modeled in MATLAB around environmental heat load, physiological inputs, phase-change materials, and a coolant loop. Because apparently putting a person inside a race car and heating them up wasn't enough.

02 / The problem

The human is
generating heat.

A race car's thermal environment is not particularly interested in whether its driver is having a good day.

The driver is dealing with heat from the surrounding environment, metabolic heat generation, protective equipment, and the thermal behavior of the cooling system itself.

That means a useful cooling system has to do more than simply make something cold. It has to manage the entire thermal balance of the person inside the system.

03 / The engineering question

So what are we
actually trying to do?

Design a cooling approach that can reduce driver thermal load while remaining lightweight, practical, and compatible with motorsport constraints.

04 / Approach

Put the physics
in the computer.

01 / THERMAL INPUTS

Account for where the heat comes from.

The model considers metabolic heat generation, environmental heat load, and the cooling contribution required to change the driver's thermal state over time.

02 / PCM BEHAVIOR

Give the heat somewhere to go.

Phase-change material provides a temporary thermal sink by absorbing energy during the phase transition, creating a cooling window before saturation.

03 / FLUID LOOP

Move the heat.

A water-glycol loop is modeled as the transport mechanism connecting the thermal source and the cooling hardware.

04 / CONTROL LOGIC

Don't cool blindly.

The concept explores changing system behavior in response to physiological and environmental inputs rather than treating the driver as a constant temperature source.

Qnet = Qmetabolic + Qenvironmental − Qcooling
Net thermal load = metabolic heat + environmental heat − heat removed by the cooling system.

06 / What I learned

Bwoah.
Thermal systems are complicated.

Cooling is a systems problem.

Changing one part of the system changes what the rest of the system has to do. Heat generation, transport, storage, and rejection cannot really be designed in isolation.

Lightweight is not the same as simple.

Reducing mass creates additional design constraints rather than removing them. Every component still has to earn its place.

The model is only as good as its assumptions.

A clean MATLAB plot is not experimental validation. Knowing what the model does not capture is part of understanding what it does.

The interesting part is the human.

Traditional thermal-system thinking becomes more interesting when the thermal system is ultimately protecting a person rather than a component.

07 / Limitations

Where the model
stops pretending.

01

Physiological heat generation is represented using modeled or assumed inputs rather than measurements from a validated human-subject experiment.

02

Environmental conditions are simplified relative to the full range of temperatures, solar load, humidity, airflow, and vehicle conditions encountered in actual motorsport.

03

PCM performance depends on material properties and real-world packaging conditions that require experimental validation.

04

The current model is a design and analysis tool, not a validated finished driver-cooling product.
